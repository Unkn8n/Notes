# Exploring Mechanisms in Everyday Machines

**Function of simple machines**

Simple mechanical systems change the magnitude or direction of force, making work easier and improving efficiency.

**Types of simple systems**

Common systems include levers, gears, pulleys, chains, and linkages used in various tasks and industries.

**Mechanical advantages and motion**

These systems increase mechanical advantages and control motion to lift loads and transmit force efficiently.

**Educational Importance**

Understanding these mechanisms is fundamental in engineering and physics education for building complex machines.

**What is a Mechanism?**

- A mechanism is a combination of machine elements (links, joints, and other components) that work together to transmit motion and force.
- It converts input motion into a desired output motion.
- It enables controlled motion and force transmission in machines.
- The choice of mechanism affects speed, efficiency, and complexity of the machine.
- It often combines multiple machine elements to achieve specific motion or force characteristics.

### Purpose of using a lever

#### 1. Gain mechanical advantage (amplify force)

A lever multiplies force, allowing a small effort to move a larger load.

Example: Using a crowbar to lift a heavy object; you push down a little, and the other end lifts something much heavier.

#### 2. Gain velocity ratio (increase motion or speed)

A lever can also make the output move farther or faster than the input.

Example: When you swing the rod or reel in, the small movements of your hands make the tip of the rod move a much larger distance and faster.

### Types of levers

#### **Class 1 levers**

#### Fulcrum Between effort and load

Class 1 levers have the fulcrum located between the applied effort and the load, allowing the direction of force to change.

#### Mechanical Advantage Variation

Adjusting the fulcrum's position closer to the load reduces required effort and increases mechanical advantage. When the fulcrum is equidistant from effort and load, equal force is applied, providing no mechanical advantage. If the fulcrum is closer to the load, less effort is needed to move it.

#### Common Examples

Class 1 levers are commonly used to increase force, depending on fulcrum placement. Typical examples include pliers and seesaws, which demonstrate directional force change and control.



### Class 2 levers

#### Class 2 lever characteristics

Class 2 levers position the load between the fulcrum and effort, multiplying force and easing heavy lifting.

The effort arm is always longer than the load arm, providing a mechanical advantage that reduces the effort required.

Class 2 levers increase force but sacrifice speed and range of motion.

Examples: wheelbarrow, nutcracker, bottle opener



### Class 3 levers

#### Class 3 lever characteristics

Class 3 levers place effort between the fulcrum and load, enhancing speed and range of motion but reducing force advantage.

The effort arm is always shorter than the load arm.

Examples: shovel, fishing rod, broom

#### How to choose the levers

Selecting lever types depends on desired benefits: force multiplication, speed, or movement control.

Use the mnemonic 'FLE' where F is fulcrum, L is load, and E is effort.

- **Class 1 (F is Middle):** The **Fulcrum** (pivot) is in the centre.
   - *Example:* A **Seesaw**. The pivot sits between the two seats.
- **Class 2 (L is Middle):** The **Load** (weight) is in the centre.
   - *Example:* A **Wheelbarrow**. The heavy load sits between the wheel and the handles.
- **Class 3 (E is Middle):** The **Effort** (force) is in the centre.
   - *Example:* A **Fishing Rod**. Your "moving hand" pushes the middle of the rod, while the other hand stays still at the bottom.
